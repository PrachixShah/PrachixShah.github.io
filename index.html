The longer version

I’m in my fourth year of Nanotechnology Engineering at Waterloo, graduating in April 2027. It’s a program that never picks a lane — one term you’re solving Schrödinger’s equation for a quantum dot, the next you’re gowning up to run photolithography, the next you’re writing a context switcher in C on a Cortex-M4. For a long time I treated that as a problem to be solved. I’ve stopped.

What actually connects it is measurement. The thing I keep being drawn to is the moment where someone says we can’t measure that directly, so we infer it — and it turns out you can, if you’re willing to think carefully about the physics for a week. At Lumentum I spent eight months on exactly that: an LCoS panel property the team had only ever estimated, turned into a real number with a real map across the panel.

I’m better in a lab than in an exam hall, and my transcript says so plainly. Lab courses land in the high 80s and low 90s; lectures don’t. I’ve made peace with that too. If you want to know whether I can do something, the honest answer is usually on the projects page rather than in a grade.

Outside of that: I take a lot of photographs of things that are too small or too orange to photograph well, I keep a list, and I’m trying to get better at writing things down while they’re still fresh instead of six months later.

Most engineers own the device, or the measurement, or the process. I got handed all three by accident and decided to keep them.
